What did the people in Singapore experience during the Japanese Occupation?

1942–1945: the British surrender, three and a half years of Japanese rule, and how ordinary people survived and responded.

6.1 · How did the Japanese establish control?

The Japanese used two broad approaches to control the people of Singapore: using force and winning loyalty.

🧩Memory hook — Force: "PIPS" — Punish, Imprison (Allied soldiers), Purge (Sook Ching), Squeeze (businessmen).
🧩Memory hook — Loyalty: the "3 P's" — Propaganda, Perks (benefits), Pride (culture).
Using force+

Meting out harsh punishments

  • Looters caught after the British surrender were executed and their heads displayed publicly (e.g. outside Cathay Building).
  • The Kempeitai (Japanese Military Police) arrested anti-Japanese suspects, who were beaten or tortured for information.

Imprisoning Allied soldiers

  • British, Australian and other Allied Europeans — including women and children — were imprisoned.
  • They were made to march publicly from Bukit Timah Road to Changi Prison.

Operation Sook Ching

  • Aimed to identify and eliminate anti-Japanese elements among locals.
  • Chinese men aged 18–50 were screened; suspected anti-Japanese individuals were killed. An estimated 25,000–50,000 people died.

Coercing Chinese businessmen

  • Chinese businessmen had earlier funded China's war effort against Japan.
  • The Overseas Chinese Association was forced to "gift" 50 million Straits dollars as punishment.
Winning loyalty+

Using propaganda

  • Portrayed themselves as liberators freeing Asians from colonial rule, via posters and radio.
  • Promoted the "Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere" — Asians free from Western control.
  • Backed the Indian National Army (led by Subhas Chandra Bose) to fight the British in India.

Providing benefits

  • Free schooling and accommodation — e.g. 20,000 students given free textbooks (1944).
  • Rice, sugar and salt distributed to the poor for Hari Raya Puasa; Malays and Indians paid lower Causeway crossing fees than the Chinese.

Imposing Japanese culture

  • Nippon-go (Japanese language) replaced English in schools, with rewards for fluency.
  • Nippon Seishin (Japanese spirit) promoted — students sang "Kimigayo" and bowed to the Emperor's portrait, and learned kendo and judo.

Explain: How did the use of force enable the Japanese to establish control over Singapore?

Point — The use of force enabled the Japanese to establish control by removing opposition and creating fear.
Evidence — Harsh punishments and Operation Sook Ching eliminated those suspected of resisting Japanese rule, while Allied soldiers were imprisoned and Chinese businessmen were financially punished.
Explain — This weakened organised resistance and removed people who could lead it. At the same time, the brutality of these actions frightened the population, making them less willing to oppose Japanese authority, while punishing businessmen ensured economic cooperation.
Link — With opposition weakened and the population intimidated into compliance, the Japanese were able to consolidate firm control over Singapore.

Explain: How did gaining the support and loyalty of the people enable the Japanese to establish control over Singapore?

Point — Winning loyalty enabled the Japanese to establish control by encouraging voluntary cooperation rather than relying only on fear.
Evidence — Propaganda presented the Japanese as liberators through the Co-Prosperity Sphere; benefits such as free textbooks and festive rations were given to locals; and Japanese culture and language were promoted through schools and competitions.
Explain — By presenting themselves as liberators and providing tangible benefits, the Japanese gave people reasons to see their rule as legitimate and even beneficial, reducing the motivation to resist. Promoting Japanese language and spirit also aimed to reshape people's identity and loyalty over time.
Link — This made some locals more willing to accept and even support Japanese rule, complementing the use of force and helping the Japanese maintain control with less active resistance.

6.2 · What was daily life like under the Japanese?

Food shortages & disease+

War disrupted trade, cutting food imports Singapore relied on. Rice, salt and sugar were rationed or sold at extremely high black-market prices.

Healthcare and sanitation worsened, and malnutrition weakened people's immune systems. Beriberi, pneumonia and dysentery were the main causes of death.

Relocations+

When growing food locally failed to solve shortages, the Japanese relocated people out of Singapore to farm elsewhere.

Endau (Johor)

  • Chinese settlers moved here.
  • Rich soil, ample water — considered a success.

Bahau (Negeri Sembilan)

  • Eurasians & Chinese Roman Catholics moved here.
  • Hilly, poor soil — people resorted to eating rats and maggots.
Leisure & entertainment+

The Japanese allowed limited leisure to maintain an appearance of a happy, harmonious society: radio programmes (Japanese songs, Malay music, Hokkien/Cantonese news), Japanese films and propaganda screenings, and sports like football, baseball, tennis and badminton — plus boxing at Kim Seng Road and police band performances at the Botanic Gardens.

Explain: Why was life difficult for ordinary people during the Japanese Occupation?

Point — Life was harsh mainly because of shortages of necessities, worsening disease, and forced relocation.
Evidence — Rice, salt and sugar were rationed or bought at high black-market prices; poor sanitation and malnutrition raised deaths from beriberi, pneumonia and dysentery; people relocated to Bahau faced poor soil and had to eat rats and maggots.
Explain — The war had disrupted trade and the food imports Singapore depended on, while wartime conditions worsened healthcare. Relocation was meant to ease shortages, but often created new suffering where the land could not support farming.
Link — These hardships show that daily survival, not just political control, defined most people's experience of the Occupation.

6.3 · How did locals respond to Japanese rule?

Collaboration and resistance+

Collaborated

  • Some Malays policed communities and reported anti-Japanese suspects.
  • Some Chinese acted as informants during Operation Sook Ching.

Resisted

  • MPAJA — worked with the British; carried out sabotage and distributed anti-Japanese materials.
  • Force 136 — secret British-organised group; member Lim Bo Seng gathered intelligence and organised sabotage.
  • Elizabeth Choy — smuggled food, money, medicine and messages to British POWs; tortured but revealed nothing.

Explain: Why did some locals collaborate with the Japanese while others resisted?

Point — People's responses varied because of different calculations about survival, safety and loyalty.
Evidence — Some Malays policed communities and some Chinese acted as informants during Sook Ching, while others — like the MPAJA, Force 136, and Elizabeth Choy — sabotaged, gathered intelligence, or aided British POWs.
Explain — Collaboration often came from a need for safety or reward, encouraged by Japanese propaganda and benefits, whereas resistance was driven by anti-Japanese sentiment, loyalty to the British, or ties to resistance networks.
Link — This shows the population's response to occupation was not uniform, but shaped by each person's circumstances and beliefs.

How did the people in Singapore respond to British rule after World War II?

From a shattered colonial myth to full internal self-government: elections, riots, and the fight for Merdeka.

7.1 · Why did people want self-government?

External developments+

Weakening of colonial rule

  • The Japanese Occupation shattered the myth of White Man's superiority.
  • Other colonies gained independence — India from Britain (1947), Indonesia from the Dutch (1949) — encouraging locals that independence was achievable.

Growing influence of communism

  • Communism's promise of equality appealed to those treated unfairly under colonial rule; China turned communist in 1949.
  • The Malayan Communist Party (MCP), remembered for resisting the Japanese, fought the British in the Malayan Emergency (1948–1960) to end colonial rule.

Complete: As a result, people in Singapore began to believe that colonial rule was...

Point — ...no longer acceptable, and that self-government was both necessary and achievable.
Evidence — The Japanese Occupation had shattered the myth of British superiority, India and Indonesia had already won independence, and the MCP was actively fighting colonial rule.
Explain — Seeing the British defeated by an Asian power, and seeing neighbouring colonies successfully gain independence, removed people's sense that colonial rule was permanent or deserved. Communism also offered an alternative, more equal vision of society.
Link — This growing loss of respect for colonial rule pushed more people in Singapore to demand self-government.

7.2 · How did the British try to win local support?

Housing, food, industry & the economy+

Housing shortages

  • The Singapore Improvement Trust (SIT) built over 20,000 housing and shop units (1947–1959).

Food shortages

  • People's Restaurants offered affordable meals — 10 by 1946.

Industrial unrest

  • 1947, the "Year of Strikes": 300+ strikes involving ~30,000 workers.
  • From 1947, trade unions had to register, letting the government monitor and restrict them.

Rebuilding the economy

  • Ports were repaired, restoring Singapore as a trading hub as demand for tin and rubber grew.

Allowing local political participation

The British introduced elections in 1948 for the Legislative Council — a first, though most of Singapore's ~960,000 people (only British subjects) were still not eligible to vote.

Explain: Why did the British try to win local support after World War II?

Point — The British acted to address local discontent and prove they were still fit to rule after their wartime defeat.
Evidence — They tackled housing (SIT built 20,000+ units), food shortages (People's Restaurants), industrial unrest (union registration laws), the economy (rebuilding ports), and allowed limited political participation (1948 elections).
Explain — After the humiliation of surrendering to Japan, the British needed to restore their legitimacy and prevent unrest — especially communist-linked strikes — from spreading, so addressing material grievances and offering limited political participation was meant to placate the population.
Link — These measures show the British responding defensively to changing local attitudes, though they still gave up very little real political power.

7.3 · Why were there riots in the early 1950s?

🧩Memory hook — the "3 R's": Religion (Maria Hertogh) → Regulation (Anti-NS) → Rights at work (Hock Lee).
Maria Hertogh Riots (1950)+

Perceived discrimination by British rulers. Maria Hertogh, a Dutch girl raised by a Muslim mother, was ordered by the courts to be returned to her birth parents and placed in a Catholic convent while awaiting return to the Netherlands; newspapers sensationalised the case.

The Muslim community felt their religion was disrespected and that the British sided with the Dutch over locals — sparking riots.

Anti-National Service Riots (1954)+

Unhappiness with government policy. The National Service Ordinance (1954) would disrupt students' education. Chinese-medium school students felt this was unfair, especially since their schools received far less support than English-medium schools.

Complete: This policy upset...

Point — ...Chinese-medium school students, who felt targeted and neglected.
Evidence — National Service would disrupt their education, and their schools already received far less support from the British than English-medium schools.
Explain — Students felt it was unjust to be conscripted to serve a government that had invested so little in their education, deepening resentment of British policy.
Link — This led secondary students from Chinese-medium schools to protest, forcing the British to close schools.
Hock Lee Bus Riots (1955)+

Poor working conditions. Workers of the Singapore Bus Workers' Union (SBWU) at Hock Lee went on strike over unfair treatment compared to a rival union; the company dismissed 229 workers instead of negotiating.

Complete: This angered the...

Point — ...bus workers and Chinese middle school students who supported them.
Evidence — 229 workers were dismissed instead of having their grievances addressed; students who distrusted the Labour Front government joined the protest.
Explain — Workers felt the company and government were not protecting their interests, while students saw it as another example of the government siding with employers over ordinary people.
Link — This anger fuelled violent clashes with police — four people were killed and many injured.

Explain: Why did riots break out in Singapore in the early 1950s?

Point — Riots broke out because of perceived discrimination, unpopular government policy, and poor working conditions.
Evidence — The Maria Hertogh Riots (1950) arose from perceived religious discrimination; the Anti-National Service Riots (1954) arose from anger at the National Service Ordinance; the Hock Lee Bus Riots (1955) arose from the unfair dismissal of workers.
Explain — In each case, a specific grievance — religious, policy-based or labour-related — combined with wider anti-colonial sentiment and distrust of British-linked authorities, causing frustration to erupt into violence.
Link — These riots reflected deepening dissatisfaction with colonial rule and pushed both the British and local leaders toward granting greater self-government.

7.4 · The Rendel Constitution & the 1955 Elections

Limited self-government+

Sir George Rendel led a commission to give locals a bigger role in government. It recommended limited self-government (1955): a Council of Ministers with a local Chief Minister and 5 other local ministers, controlling education, health, housing, trade and industry — while the British kept control of internal security and foreign affairs.

David Marshall (Labour Front) became Singapore's 1st Chief Minister, leading a coalition with the Alliance Party.

The 1948 elections+

The British allowed some locals to be elected into the Legislative Council (which made laws and discussed government policies) — 6 seats were up for election.

  • Only British subjects were eligible to vote; registration was not automatic; voting was not compulsory.
  • About 14,000 voters took part in total.
  • The Singapore Progressive Party won 3 seats; Independents won the other 3.

  • Expand free medical services.
  • Build low-cost housing, clear slums.
  • Keep income tax unchanged.
  • Malayanise the Civil Service.
  • Homes for underprivileged families.

Labour Front's campaign promises

  • Immediate self-government.
  • Citizenship for permanent residents.
  • Repeal Emergency Regulations.
  • Build a welfare state; low-interest loans.
  • Clear slums; more hospitals/clinics.
  • Financial aid for the unemployed; minimum wage.

Journey Towards Merdeka

The Hock Lee Bus Riots & Marshall's dilemma+

Both Lee Kuan Yew (legal advisor to SBWU) and Fong Swee Suan (linked to the riots) were connected to the Legislative Assembly — showing how entangled politics and the unions were.

Marshall did not want to use force on rioters but also needed to restore order. He eventually negotiated an agreement between the unions and Hock Lee Bus Company — dismissed workers got their jobs back. Because he was sympathetic to the rioters and avoided strong action against communist leaders, the British viewed him unfavourably.

First Merdeka Talks (1956) — failed+

Marshall launched Merdeka Week (12 March 1956) and led talks in London, aiming for full internal self-government by 1957 and greater control over defence and external affairs. He vowed to resign if talks failed.

Why the talks failed

  • The British were unimpressed by Marshall's weak handling of the Hock Lee Bus Riots.
  • They doubted the Labour Front's ability to maintain order and deal with the communist threat.
  • Britain wanted a strong, stable government to protect its economic and defence interests.

True to his word, Marshall resigned as Chief Minister.

Lim Yew Hock & the Chinese Middle School Students' Riots (1956)+

Lim Yew Hock succeeded Marshall as 2nd Chief Minister. His goals: crush the communist threat (where Marshall had failed) and gain British trust by proving Singapore could control communism.

His government banned the Singapore Chinese Middle School Students' Union, sparking protests. When demonstrations turned violent, Malayan troops were called in and students were tear-gassed. The riots lasted 3 days, killed 13 people, and over 900 were arrested — including union leaders and PAP members Lim Chin Siong and Fong Swee Suan.

Second Merdeka Talks (1957) — success+

Led by Lim Yew Hock, aiming for full control over internal matters including internal security.

Why the talks succeeded

  • The British were pleased with Lim Yew Hock's decisive handling of the riots.
  • He showed willingness to compromise and recognised British concerns about communism.

The talks secured full internal self-government for Singapore.

Explain: Why did the First Merdeka Talks (1956) fail while the Second Merdeka Talks (1957) succeeded?

Point — The outcome of each round of talks depended on whether the British trusted the Singapore government to control internal security, especially the communist threat.
Evidence — Marshall's weak handling of the Hock Lee Bus Riots left the British doubting the Labour Front's ability to maintain order, so the First Talks failed; Lim Yew Hock's firm handling of the 1956 riots and willingness to compromise convinced the British Singapore was ready, so the Second Talks succeeded.
Explain — The British were mainly concerned with protecting their economic and defence interests and preventing communism from taking hold; only a leader who demonstrated firm control could earn enough trust to be handed internal security.
Link — This comparison shows Britain's willingness to grant self-government was tied directly to its confidence in local leaders' ability to manage internal order.

  • The 1957 Citizenship Ordinance gave ~320,000 residents (including 220,000 foreign-born Chinese) the right to vote.
  • 525,000 voters in total — a huge jump from 160,000 in 1955.

In 1959, internal self-government began with the PAP forming the government and Lee Kuan Yew as Singapore's first Prime Minister. The National Flag, State Crest, National Anthem and new Head of State (Yusof Ishak) were unveiled on 3 December 1959.

Explain: Why did the PAP win the 1959 election by such a large margin?

Point — The PAP won overwhelmingly both because its main rival collapsed and because the PAP itself ran a strong, popular campaign.
Evidence — The Singapore People's Alliance (formerly Labour Front) was seen as too close to the British after the 1956 riots and was hit by a corruption scandal involving Education Minister Chew Swee Kee; its campaign was also poorly organised. Meanwhile, the PAP had charismatic leadership under Lee Kuan Yew, popular Chinese-speaking figures like Lim Chin Siong and Fong Swee Suan who appealed to workers and students, and a well-planned campaign.
Explain — Voters who wanted genuine anti-colonial leadership and integrity turned away from the Alliance's compromised image, while the PAP's clear vision and its leaders' popularity — especially among Chinese-speaking voters — gave it broad-based support across communities.
Link — This combination of a discredited rival and strong, well-organised PAP leadership resulted in the PAP capturing 43 of 51 seats.

Merger with Malaya

Why did the PAP pursue merger?+

To gain political independence from the British

  • Merger was seen as the only way to gain independence; the British were reluctant to let Singapore go because of the Cold War.
  • Britain feared Southeast Asia falling to communism and wanted a new federation (Malaya, Singapore, North Borneo, Sarawak) to keep the region politically stable.
  • The British believed Malaya could help suppress communism and protect their interests, so the PAP judged that independence could only come through joining this federation.

To ensure economic survival

  • Singapore lacked natural resources, had a fast-growing population needing jobs, and faced declining entrepôt trade.
  • Malayan tariffs on Singapore-made goods reduced trade between the two.
  • Merger promised a larger economic base and a common market — untaxed access to the Malayan hinterland, more demand for goods, and more jobs.

How a tariff worked: a $10 pair of Singapore-made shoes cost $12.50 in Malaya once a 25% tariff was added — making Singapore goods less competitive there.

"It is the hinterland which produces the rubber and tin that keep our shop-window economy going... Without merger... our economic position will slowly and steadily get worse." — adapted from Lee Kuan Yew's The Battle for Merger radio talks, 1961

Explain: Why did the PAP government pursue merger with Malaya?

Point — The PAP pursued merger mainly for political independence and economic survival.
Evidence — Politically, the British would only consider granting independence within a new anti-communist federation. Economically, Singapore lacked resources, faced declining entrepôt trade, and suffered from Malayan tariffs on its goods.
Explain — Since the British linked independence to being part of a stable, communism-resistant federation, merger was the only realistic route to full self-rule. At the same time, joining Malaya's larger economy through a common market would remove tariffs, boost trade and create jobs, solving Singapore's long-term economic problems.
Link — Merger therefore offered the PAP a way to achieve both political independence and economic security at the same time.
Malaya vs. Malaysia — and who was Tunku Abdul Rahman?+

Malaya ≠ Malaysia

Malaya was the British-colonial name for the region (Peninsular Malaysia + Singapore) until independence in 1957. Malaysia is the name of the new country formed in 1963, when Malaya joined with Sabah and Sarawak — and, until 1965, Singapore.

Tunku Abdul Rahman

  • 7th son of the Sultan of Kedah.
  • Studied Law & History at Cambridge University, graduating in 1925.
  • Joined the Kedah Civil Service, later became a Deputy Public Prosecutor (1949).
  • Became President of UMNO (1951) and Chief Minister of the Federation of Malaya (1955).
  • Supervised Malaya's independence process, achieved on 31 August 1957.
Malaya's initial response & why Tunku changed his mind+

Tunku Abdul Rahman was initially reluctant

  • Worried Singapore's mostly Chinese population might resist a Malay Sultan, the Malay language, or Islam as state religion.
  • Feared too many Chinese would upset Malaya's racial balance.
  • Suspected many Chinese were sympathetic to communists, recalling the tough fight during the Emergency.

Why he changed his mind

  • Growing communism: Tunku feared the PAP could be taken over by pro-communist members, producing a Singapore government unwilling to agree to merger on Malaya's terms. He realised Singapore would be safer under Malaysian control.
  • Split within the PAP: Ong Eng Guan, expelled from PAP for pro-communist leanings, won the Hong Lim by-election as an independent. This alarmed Tunku that radicals might take over the PAP and turn Singapore communist, so he agreed to merger to help suppress communism.

Explain: Why did Tunku Abdul Rahman change his mind and support merger with Singapore?

Point — Tunku's support grew out of fear that Singapore could become a base for communism if left outside Malaya's control.
Evidence — He worried the PAP might be taken over by pro-communist members, and Ong Eng Guan's expulsion from the PAP followed by his win in the Hong Lim by-election alarmed Tunku about a possible communist takeover of the party.
Explain — Tunku reasoned that bringing Singapore into the federation would let the Malaysian government directly control and suppress communist elements there, rather than risk communism spreading from an unstable, independent Singapore into Malaya.
Link — His shift shows that Cold War fears of communism, more than goodwill toward Singapore, ultimately drove Malaya to accept merger.
Opposition: the Barisan Sosialis+

Lim Chin Siong and Fong Swee Suan felt merger might disadvantage Singapore and questioned whether Singaporeans would get equal rights to Malayans. They split from the PAP to form the Barisan Sosialis on 13 Aug 1961 (Dr Lee Siew Choh, Chairman; Lim Chin Siong, Secretary-General) — demanding a full, unreserved merger to prevent discrimination.

Contest for merger: PAP vs Barisan Sosialis+

PAP's campaign

  • Year-long campaign to win support for its version of merger.
  • LKY's 12 radio talks ("The Battle for Merger", Sep–Oct 1961) in English, Malay and Mandarin — reaching Singapore, Brunei, Malaya, North Borneo and Sarawak.
  • Exhibitions (e.g. "We the People's of Malaysia") showcased close cultural and social ties.

Barisan Sosialis's campaign

  • Speeches at mass rallies to win support.
  • Accepted invitations to debate merger issues in radio forums alongside other parties.
The people's decision: the 1962 Referendum+

A referendum was held on 1 Sep 1962. The PAP urged voters to choose Option A (its version of merger); the Barisan Sosialis rejected all three options and urged blank votes in protest.

Explain: What was the significance of the 1962 referendum results?

Point — The referendum results showed that most Singaporeans backed the PAP's vision of merger over the Barisan Sosialis's opposition.
Evidence — 71% voted for Option A (the PAP's proposed terms), while 25% cast blank votes as the Barisan Sosialis had urged, and only a small share chose Options B or C.
Explain — The strong majority for Option A gave the PAP a clear mandate to proceed with merger on its own terms, while the sizeable blank-vote share revealed that a meaningful minority still distrusted or opposed the PAP's approach.
Link — The result legitimised the PAP's push for merger, and together with Operation Coldstore, effectively weakened the Barisan Sosialis's political influence.
Terms of merger (16 Sep 1963)+
  • Singapore could hold its own elections to choose its state government.
  • Singapore's state government controlled education and labour.
  • The Central Government (Kuala Lumpur) controlled defence, foreign affairs and internal security.
  • All Singapore citizens automatically became Malaysian citizens but could not vote in Malaysian elections.

Reasons for Separation

Singapore was part of Malaysia for less than two years. Economic disagreements and political differences between the Federal Government and Singapore's state government led to separation on 9 August 1965.

Economic disagreements+

Revenue contribution

Singapore had agreed to contribute 40% of its revenue to the Federal government. The Federal government later raised this to 60%, citing increased defence spending due to Konfrontasi — which Singapore felt was unfair.

The common market

Singapore expected tariffs on its goods to be removed, but the Federal Government delayed setting up the common market, continuing to protect Malayan industries with tariffs.

Political disagreements+

Different political beliefs

The PAP was multiracial, believing in representing all communities equally. The Federal government (the Alliance Party — UMNO, MCA, MIC) aimed to protect the rights of each party's own race.

Different treatment of races

The Federal government gave Malays special privileges in jobs, business and education (e.g. reserved government jobs, free education) to improve their welfare. The PAP government objected to this special treatment.

The 1964 Racial Riots

What happened+

On 21 July 1964, an estimated 20,000 Muslims gathered at the Padang to commemorate Prophet Muhammad's birthday. Clashes broke out during the procession along Geylang Road and Guillemard Road and spread as news travelled. 23 people died and 454 were injured.

Causes of the riots+

Political differences in the treatment of races

The PAP wanted a multicultural Malaysia treating all races equally; UMNO believed Malays should get special privileges. Some UMNO leaders accused the PAP of neglecting Malay interests via the newspaper Utusan Melayu, creating tension between Malays and Chinese.

Rivalry between PAP and UMNO

After merger, both parties contested each other's elections. The Alliance Party failed to win any seat in Singapore's 1963 elections, even in Malay-dominated constituencies, and the PAP's participation in the 1964 Federal Elections further strained relations. UMNO leaders in Singapore stirred anti-PAP feelings during the Prophet's birthday celebrations, helping spark the riots.

Explain: Why did the 1964 racial riots break out?

Point — The riots broke out because of political differences over racial treatment and rivalry between the PAP and UMNO.
Evidence — The PAP's multiracial stance clashed with UMNO's belief in Malay privileges, with Utusan Melayu accusing the PAP of neglecting Malay interests; the two parties had also been contesting each other's elections since merger, raising tensions ahead of the Prophet Muhammad's birthday procession on 21 July 1964.
Explain — These political and electoral rivalries stirred up communal anxieties, so when speeches attacking the PAP were made during the religious procession, existing racial and political tension erupted into violence.
Link — The riots convinced Tunku that the differences between Singapore and the Federal Government were irreconcilable, pushing the two governments toward eventual separation.

Explain: Why did Singapore separate from Malaysia?

Point — Singapore separated from Malaysia because economic and political disagreements between the Federal and State governments became irreconcilable, culminating in the 1964 racial riots.
Evidence — Economically, the Federal government raised Singapore's revenue contribution from 40% to 60% and delayed the common market. Politically, the PAP's multiracial stance clashed with the Alliance's Malay-privilege policies, and rivalry between the PAP and UMNO in each other's elections fed into the racial riots of July 1964, which killed 23 and injured 454.
Explain — These economic disputes made Singapore feel it was being treated unfairly within the federation, while the political and racial tensions convinced Tunku Abdul Rahman that differences between the two governments could not be resolved and that further unrest would spread across the Federation.
Link — Believing separation was the only way to prevent further disorder, Tunku moved for Singapore to leave — and on 9 August 1965, Lee Kuan Yew announced Singapore's separation, with the Independence of Singapore Agreement making Singapore a fully independent nation.
Separation — 9 August 1965+

The growing unrest and disagreement convinced Tunku that the best resolution was for Singapore to leave Malaysia. On Monday, 9 August 1965, live on television, Prime Minister Lee Kuan Yew announced that Singapore was no longer part of Malaysia. With the signing of the Independence of Singapore Agreement, Singapore became an independent nation.
